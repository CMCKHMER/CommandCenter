Reading Passage (3 minutes to read)

The Decline of the Maya Civilization

The collapse of the Classic Maya civilization in the 9th century AD has long puzzled archaeologists. Several theories have been proposed to explain why this advanced society suddenly abandoned its great cities in the southern lowlands.

First, many researchers believe that severe droughts were the primary cause. Geological evidence from lake sediments suggests that the region experienced prolonged periods of little rainfall during the time of the collapse. Without sufficient water, agriculture would have failed, leading to famine and social unrest.

Second, some scholars argue that overpopulation and environmental degradation played a key role. As the population grew, the Maya cleared vast forests for farming and construction. This deforestation likely led to soil erosion and a decrease in agricultural productivity, making it impossible to sustain the large urban centers.

Finally, another theory suggests that constant warfare between city-states caused the collapse. Evidence of fortifications and violent destruction in various sites indicates that competition for resources led to endless conflicts, destabilizing the political structure and causing people to flee.

Lecture Transcript

(Listen to the professor discuss the reading passage...)

Professor: While the reading passage offers three plausible theories for the Maya collapse, recent archaeological findings suggest that the reality was more complex and that these factors might not be the sole causes.

First, regarding the drought theory, while it is true that there were dry periods, new evidence shows that the Maya had sophisticated water storage systems. They built reservoirs and canals that could sustain them through moderate droughts. The collapse happened in areas where water management was actually quite effective, suggesting drought alone wasn't enough to destroy them.

Second, about overpopulation and soil erosion. Recent studies of soil samples indicate that the Maya used sustainable farming techniques, such as terracing and crop rotation. In fact, some areas show signs of intensive agriculture that remained productive for centuries. The environmental damage might have been localized rather than widespread.

Lastly, concerning warfare. Although there is evidence of conflict, it appears that warfare was a constant feature of Maya life throughout their history, not just during the collapse. If war were the main cause, the civilization would have collapsed much earlier. Instead, trade networks remained active even during the decline, suggesting that political instability, rather than total war, was the issue.

Professor's Question

Dr. Gupta: This week we are discussing the impact of artificial intelligence on the job market. Some experts argue that AI will eliminate millions of jobs, leading to high unemployment. Others believe that AI will create new types of jobs and increase productivity. What is your opinion? Will AI have a net positive or negative effect on employment in the future?

Claire

I think the impact will be largely negative. We are already seeing automation replace workers in manufacturing and customer service. Even skilled jobs like accounting and legal analysis are at risk. I worry that the new jobs created will require skills that displaced workers don't have, leading to long-term structural unemployment.

Raj

I disagree. Historically, technology has always created more jobs than it destroyed. The invention of the computer eliminated some clerical work but created the entire tech industry. AI will handle boring, repetitive tasks, freeing humans to focus on creative and strategic work. We will see a boom in industries we can't even imagine yet.
